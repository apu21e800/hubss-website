The spray park at Maple Ridge Park in Maple Ridge, British Columbia, opened in July 2025 with its design laid on the pad in blue and orange [StreetBond](/products/streetbond), a salmon graphic among the colour fields. The Product Portfolio Book · Volume 5 files it under public art, with the line "The playground is the canvas." For landscape architects drawing parks and plazas, this guide follows the HUB Surface Systems materials that put a design like that on the ground, each through a documented job, with what to specify.

## StreetBond at Maple Ridge and Snug Cove

A [splash pad](/applications/splash-pads) stays wet all season under bare feet and chemical treatment, and the book asks for a surface that is slip-resistant and cool underfoot. StreetBond is a pavement coating, a water-based, epoxy-modified acrylic for asphalt and concrete, and on a splash pad it goes down in a textured, slip-resistant build. It is designed to move with the pavement, which the book says avoids "the three failures of rigid coatings: cracking, premature wear, and being slippery in wet conditions." It comes in 63 standard colours or a custom match, with a service life of 8+ years, and it is easily refreshed: a worn pad is recoated between seasons.

At Snug Cove on Bowen Island, British Columbia, the same coating makes a path into coloured asphalt with a sense of humour: four custom colours representing Forest, Sunset, Water and Earth, and caricatures of local fauna worked into the design. [The Snug Cove write-up](/blog/bowen-island-asphalt-path) gives the reason it holds on a path that carries art: the coating works with the normal expansion and contraction of the pavement, and it "will not peel, delaminate or shrink-crack" when installed to its specifications.

## A labyrinth on the hospital deck

At BC Children's Hospital in Vancouver, British Columbia, the outdoor spaces off the wards on several floors and the ground-level plaza carry StreetBond and [DecoMark](/products/decomark), to a hardscape design by Connect Landscape Architecture. On the deck of one level the StreetBond forms a labyrinth, and the DecoMark icons represent female animals native to BC and their young. The book gives the job a spread of its own, pages 94 and 95.

StreetBond went onto new acid-etched concrete. The DecoMark icons went onto the concrete, and onto precast concrete tiles off-site; the tiles were then delivered, lifted to the floors and placed, which the project's write-up says "allowed for efficient installation and high-quality control". Square One Paving installed the work, and the book adds that the surfaces are slip-resistant and chemical-resistant, built to perform in a healthcare environment.

Oren Mizrahi, Associate, BCSLA, CSLA, LEED AP, of Connect Landscape Architecture worked with HUB from the design phase through installation. He said HUB "worked closely with us throughout the project providing sound technical direction along the way and the net result was a successful implementation and translation of our design ideas". [The BC Children's write-up](/blog/bc-childrens-hospital-labyrinth) has the job in full.

## Placemaking on a Toronto park path

At Geary Avenue Park in Toronto, Ontario, the path carries the Geary Works name. The name and its gear motif read straight off the pavement as you walk it, in DecoMark, with no plinth and no interpretive panel, and the book files the job under [Parks & paths](/applications/parks-paths), as community branding (page 97).

[The Geary Works write-up](/blog/geary-works-toronto-park-walkway) sets out how it is done. The artwork is made to specification first, its colour chosen from the palette or matched before anything is made, then heat-applied in one operation, bonding to the asphalt. The path is then maintained as pavement, on the cycle it was already on, rather than as a separate asset with its own replacement schedule. For a parks department, the write-up says, "that distinction is often what decides whether a placemaking idea survives the capital review."

## Public art across an intersection in Kitchener

At King and Queen Streets in downtown Kitchener, Ontario, [public art](/applications/public-art) makes a historic intersection a place for people on foot. The book shows it from above on page 107, under the eyebrow Intersection art and the title "Kings and Queens."

It is [TrafficPatterns](/products/traffic-patterns), the preformed thermoplastic the book's public art page specifies as "large-scale interconnected thermoplastic". The artwork is made up in the factory to the design, laid out on site and heat-applied, so each sheet bonds to the asphalt or concrete beneath it. The sheet is 125 mil thick and grips at 60 BPN on the ASTM E303 test, and the book gives TrafficPatterns 8+ years of service life.

## StreetBondSR and the urban heat island

Dark asphalt absorbs most of the sun's energy and adds to the urban heat island, the measurable temperature difference between a city and the rural areas around it. Twelve [StreetBondSR](/products/streetbondsr) colours reflect sunlight rather than absorb it, so asphalt stays cooler underfoot. Each carries an initial solar reflectance of 0.33 or higher, which can count toward the LEED v5 [heat island credit](/applications/leed-urban-heat-island) for surfaces other than roofs, and the coating goes on asphalt, with a service life of 8+ years.

The photograph beside the book's StreetBondSR page is a Canada Post fleet lot in Toronto, Ontario: parking, the first use that page lists. For a park path, the book's then-and-now pages show StreetBondSR at Jack Shaw Gardens in Osoyoos, British Columbia, when it went in and three years later, when the path "still reads as new, and runs cooler underfoot" ([the Osoyoos write-up](/blog/streetbondsr-solar-reflective-coatings)).

Specify StreetBond colours by name: the book's colour pages call the swatches indicative and ask for a physical sample for colour approval. On concrete, the coating goes onto acid-etched concrete, as it did at BC Children's Hospital. DecoMark is cut from vector artwork, so the design travels as vector files.
