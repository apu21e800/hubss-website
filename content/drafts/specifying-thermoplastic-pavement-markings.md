On Woodbridge Avenue in Vaughan, Ontario, the City chose [TrafficPatternsXD](/products/traffic-patterns-xd) for every crosswalk at an intersection, because snowplow blades catch and displace real brick pavers. A choice like that ends up as lines in a tender: the system named, the street it suits, the documents behind it and the crew that installs it. This guide goes through those lines for thermoplastic pavement markings and the transit lanes beside them, with the Canadian jobs behind each one.

## The crosswalks that survive the plow

The Village of Woodbridge is a designated Heritage Conservation District, and when the City of Vaughan undertook a major streetscape improvement of Woodbridge Avenue, the crosswalk specification became one of the most challenging details on the project: brick pavers at intersections leave raised edges that plow blades catch and displace. In HUB Surface Systems' [write-up of the Woodbridge job](/blog/trafficpatternsxd-urban-design), Shirley Marsh, Project Manager of Urban Design for the City of Vaughan, says the City "chose TrafficPatternsXD for all pedestrian crosswalks at intersections". Of the project's goals, among them material durability and efficient operations and maintenance, she says the team "successfully achieved these goals in the completion of the Woodbridge Avenue streetscape improvement project." Thermo Design installed the crosswalks.

TrafficPatternsXD is aggregate-reinforced preformed thermoplastic, 150 mil thick, heated on prepared asphalt and stamped through into the top of it, so the brick look comes from texture and colour, with no raised edge and no grout joint for a blade to catch. Its aggregate, rated 8–9 on the Mohs scale, runs through the full cross-section. The Product Portfolio Book · Volume 5 gives it a skid resistance of 60 BPN (ASTM E303) and a service life of 10+ years, and heads its Woodbridge page "Plowed, salted, and heavy traffic." On [page 40](/product-book/40), another Woodbridge photograph has a plow working the red brick pattern with its blade down.

In Toronto's Emery Village the pavers were already in. The Emery Village Business Improvement Area (BIA) had laid coloured unit pavers in the community's "Emery Blue" as a gateway feature and traffic calming measure, and they broke down under heavy commercial and truck traffic; patching them was only ever a temporary fix. In 2020, twelve years after they went in, the BIA set out to replace them, and TrafficPatternsXD took their place: 440 m² in the same colour and the same paver grid, installed by Multiseal. Al Ruggero, Project Manager with Emery Business BIA, gives the reason in [the Emery Village write-up](/blog/decorative-asphalt-high-traffic): "The material provided us with continuity of design and less traffic disruption in construction than with replacing the pavers or demolishing the concrete base." [The guide to snowplow-safe decorative pavement](/blog/snowplow-safe-decorative-pavement) sets out what makes any decorative surface plow-safe.

## Medians in Verdun and a crosswalk in Georgina

In Verdun, Québec, TrafficPatternsXD makes flush medians. The book's photograph on [page 116](/product-book/116) shows a curved median in a grey paver pattern edged in white, on the existing road surface, and the book calls it "visual and tactile traffic control": a "snowplow-proof, road-narrowing installation" that slows cars and protects pedestrians. Its Traffic calming spread gives the reason: "Changing colour and texture tells a driver the environment has changed before any sign does."

In York Region's Town of Georgina, Ontario, the Town wanted a crosswalk that honours the Every Child Matters movement. It is [TrafficPatterns](/products/traffic-patterns) in a custom design: orange, with "every child matters" in white script, in the photograph in [the Georgina write-up](/blog/every-child-matters-crosswalk). TrafficPatterns is the 125 mil preformed thermoplastic, factory-made to the design and heat-applied to asphalt or concrete, where it bonds to the surface. Its anti-skid aggregate runs through the full cross-section, skid resistance is 60 BPN (ASTM E303), it opens to traffic within hours, and its service life is 8+ years. The write-up gives the reason for it: surface-applied and virtually maintenance-free, it is a cost-effective alternative to brick pavers.

The book draws the line between the two sheets itself: "TrafficPatternsXD steps up to a 150 mil aggregate-reinforced build for the hardest-working surfaces: intersections, transit corridors, loading aprons." TrafficPatterns is its sheet for custom designs, on concrete as well as asphalt.

## Bus lanes in MMAX, marked overnight

On key TTC bus priority corridors in Toronto, the red lanes are [MMAX](/products/mmax), put down in overnight installations with no long closures. [The TTC case study](/blog/extending-transit-lane-lifespan) records fewer repainting cycles over more than five years, and lanes that stay visible in winter and in the wet.

MMAX is a methyl methacrylate (MMA) area marking: a two-part resin with hard aggregate and colourfast pigment, for red bus lanes, green bike lanes and transit priority lanes. The book gives its cure as 45–60 minutes to traffic-ready and its minimum installation temperature as +3°C. The first figure lets an active corridor be marked in one overnight closure; the second matters in a Canadian season. The book's London pages say where a bus lane wears: "Bus lanes fail where the loads concentrate: stop pads, turning movements, stations."

On [the East London Link](/blog/london-east-link-brt) in London, Ontario, the two systems went in together: red MMAX in the bus lanes and TrafficPatternsXD at the crosswalks, "one corridor, two materials, each doing the job it is built for," in the book's words. HUB's case studies say the corridor supported London's Complete Streets goals. The transit jobs in York Region and Waterloo Region are in [the guide for public works leaders](/blog/white-paper-resilient-transit-infrastructure) and [the technical resources for engineering teams](/blog/transportation-infrastructure-guide).

## Writing thermoplastic pavement markings into the tender

The lines worth writing:

- **The system, by name:** TrafficPatternsXD, TrafficPatterns or MMAX, with the book's spec line for each, as above. Where the tender cannot name a product, use the generic TrafficPatternsXD specification.
- **The surface:** TrafficPatternsXD goes on asphalt only; TrafficPatterns goes on asphalt or concrete.
- **The street:** intersections with heavy, turning traffic and the crosswalks on a transit corridor take TrafficPatternsXD; a crosswalk made to a community's design takes TrafficPatterns; the bus lane takes MMAX.
- **The window:** MMAX goes down from +3°C and is traffic-ready in 45–60 minutes; TrafficPatterns opens to traffic within hours. Ask HUB how long a TrafficPatternsXD section stays closed.
- **The colour:** approve it from a physical sample, available by request for the thermoplastic colours; the MMAX colour palette is on its page.
- **The installer:** "Installed by a HUB-certified installer to manufacturer specification", which makes the installation method part of what is bid.
