In London, Ontario, the City of London put [TrafficPatternsXD](/products/traffic-patterns-xd) at the crossings of the [East London Link](/blog/london-east-link-brt), where buses brake and turn, and red [MMAX](/products/mmax) on its transit lanes. HUB specifies the same pair for bus rapid transit (BRT) corridors, because the crossings and the lanes of a corridor wear in different ways. This guide takes a corridor system by system, with the jobs that show each one in service.

## How a bus rapid transit corridor wears

The transit corridor page of the Product Portfolio Book · Volume 5 says such corridors "concentrate wear into a few square metres." On a bus rapid transit corridor those square metres are easy to find. At a crossing the wear comes from buses braking and turning on the same patch of road, and a crosswalk under that traffic wears out by losing its contrast and its grip. In the lanes the loads gather at the stop pads, the turning movements and the stations, and what a transit lane has to keep is its colour: one continuous field that a driver reads, with no sign needed, as a lane for the buses. A lane also has to go in without shutting the corridor down, so the closure a road can give counts as much as the material.

The corridors below answer those two kinds of wear with two systems, and a third carries the markings a driver has to read at night.

## TrafficPatternsXD at the crossings

The Product Portfolio Book calls TrafficPatternsXD the system "for the toughest environments": aggregate-reinforced preformed thermoplastic, 150 mil thick, "proven in high traffic with concentrated wheel loads." Its aggregate rates 8–9 on the Mohs scale and is mixed through the full thickness of the sheet, so the grip does not wear off with the top layer; as the surface wears, fresh aggregate comes to the top. The book's figures for it are 60 BPN of skid resistance (ASTM E303) and 10+ years of service life.

It goes on asphalt only. The sheets are laid on the prepared asphalt and heated, then a template is vibrated into the melted material, keying the pattern into the asphalt below. On the East London Link it went in at the crosswalks and at the conflict points where buses, cyclists and pedestrians meet, since the corridor also runs [bike lanes beside the buses](/blog/cycling-transit-integration-surface-solutions). ## Crossings in Richmond, Waterloo Region, York Region and Kelowna

At [TransLink's Brighouse Station](/blog/richmond-brighouse-crosswalk) in Richmond, British Columbia, TrafficPatternsXD crosswalks run across No. 3 Road and through the development area. The work needed crossings that would hold up under high-traffic road conditions and stay skid-resistant. The write-up notes that a material stamped into the asphalt surface itself does not settle unevenly and leaves no trip edge. The Product Portfolio Book shows the Richmond crossings on its page about pedestrian channelization, "keeping pedestrians in a clear, defined space."

The [ION LRT in Waterloo Region](/blog/safety-durability-transit-stations), Ontario, is light rail transit, and its crossings take the same system. On the ION corridor, TrafficPatternsXD is at the platform edges, the crosswalks and the transfer points, put down in phased night-time applications that did not disrupt service. The write-up records crossings that kept their contrast through the freeze-thaw cycles and gave better traction in winter.

York Region's Highway 7 rapidway, a bus rapid transit corridor on the VIVA network designed under the Complete Streets framework, has TrafficPatternsXD at intersections along the corridor: at the crosswalks and the bus stop platforms, and at the points where people change between bike, bus and foot. [The York Region case study](/blog/multimodal-connectivity-york-region) records crossings that stayed visible through several winters, and the Product Portfolio Book's page on them carries the headline "Service life in years, not seasons."

Kelowna, British Columbia, has built [a downtown crosswalk network](/blog/performance-crosswalks-asphalt-concrete) in TrafficPatternsXD. When HUB wrote it up, the city had installed its first TrafficPatternsXD crosswalk 13 years earlier and more than 80 since, through the city core, with more planned. Its aim, in the write-up's words, was streets that serve "a broader range of transportation modes, with a focus on pedestrian safety, cyclists and transit service."

## Red MMAX on the transit lanes

The Product Portfolio Book calls bus lanes "the hardest duty a road surface sees: fully loaded buses braking, turning and pulling in at the same spot, all day, every day," over a photograph of red MMAX in London, Ontario. MMAX is methyl methacrylate with embedded aggregate for grip, a two-part resin that cures cold, and the book gives it the two figures a corridor schedule turns on: traffic-ready in 45–60 minutes, and a minimum installation temperature of +3°C.

On the East London Link, MMAX is the red of the dedicated transit lanes, laid as one continuous coloured lane; it is what reads from the curb as red asphalt. With that cure time, a stretch of lane goes in and carries buses again the same shift, without the disruption of a multi-day closure, and because it goes down from +3°C, it lengthens the working season on an Ontario project. It holds its colour at the stop pads and turning movements where bus lanes fail.

Toronto has MMAX on key TTC bus priority corridors, applied in overnight installations. [The guide to transit lanes and crosswalks in London and Toronto](/blog/durable-transit-lanes-crossings) and [the case study on transit lane service life](/blog/extending-transit-lane-lifespan) cover both cities, and the [bus lanes page](/applications/bus-lanes) sets out where each system goes.

## PreMark for the markings drivers read at night

[PreMark](/products/premark) is preformed thermoplastic for [regulatory markings](/applications/regulatory-markings), pre-cut to specification: arrows, stop bars, yield triangles, legends, bike symbols and ladder lines. The Product Portfolio Book gives it at 125 mil, with integrated glass beads for night visibility and a service life of 6–8 years. It is heat-applied by torch and bonds to the surface, with no stencils and no curing window, so traffic drives on it straight away.

TrafficPatternsXD gives a crosswalk its contrast and its grip, and it carries no glass beads: it is decorative and anti-skid. Where a marking has to send a driver's headlights back at night, as a stop bar or a legend does, the beads in PreMark do that work. The book's [pedestrian safety](/applications/pedestrian-safety) page specifies both systems, for "high-contrast materials, combined with retroreflective markings that stay visible year round." For a bike lane running beside the buses, PreMark also supplies the symbols, arrows and bike boxes, as the [bike lanes](/applications/bike-lanes) page sets out.

## Specifying a corridor system by system

| Where on the corridor | System | How it goes in | The book's figures |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Crosswalks, conflict points and platforms | TrafficPatternsXD | Heated and stamped into the asphalt | 150 mil, 60 BPN (ASTM E303), 10+ years |
| The transit lanes | MMAX | Methyl methacrylate, cured cold | Traffic-ready in 45–60 minutes, from +3°C |
| Stop bars, arrows, legends and symbols | PreMark | Heat-applied by torch, bonds to the surface | 125 mil, glass beads, 6–8 years |

The East London Link's write-up ends on the two questions worth settling early: how long the corridor can be held for installation, and where on the alignment the wear concentrates. Those two answers usually choose the materials.
