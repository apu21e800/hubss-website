Two [TrafficPatterns](/products/traffic-patterns) crosswalks carry a First Nations land acknowledgement outside the Kwanlin Dün Cultural Centre on the Whitehorse waterfront, Yukon. HUB Surface Systems prints them on [page 113 of the Product Portfolio Book · Volume 5](/product-book/113), under "Acknowledgement, cast in pavement."

## Two crosswalks outside the centre

The photograph is taken at road level, with two orange crosswalks running across the road in front of the centre. The nearer one carries two large white feathers, laid along its length, and a second crosswalk farther off carries a line of white lettering. Behind them are the centre's dark, gabled buildings, its name in letters on one wall, and a planted island of young trees.

## Heated onto the road in sheets

TrafficPatterns is preformed thermoplastic. In the factory the design is made up in sheets 125 mil thick (about 3 mm); on site the sheets are laid on the pavement and heated until they bond to it. The pavement itself is not cut or stamped, and a crosswalk in TrafficPatterns is open to traffic within hours.

## The grip is in the material

Grip comes from anti-skid aggregate mixed into the full thickness of the sheet, so it is still there as the surface wears. The book rates the skid resistance at 60 BPN under ASTM E303 and gives TrafficPatterns a service life of 8+ years.

## Another land acknowledgement in the book

On page 106 the book shows a second job of the same kind in TrafficPatterns, at the University of British Columbia, where [the UBC and Musqueam crests are woven together in one crosswalk](/blog/ubc-musqueam-crosswalk). The system itself is one of those specified on the book's [community branding](/applications/community-branding) spread, for streetscape work, under the line "Every neighbourhood has a story."
