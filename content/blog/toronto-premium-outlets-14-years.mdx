# Fourteen years, and still the path people take

Halton Hills, Ontario

Retail parking lots are a harder environment than most people credit. They combine the things that destroy surface markings — concentrated turning movements, delivery vehicles, salt, plows — with the one thing a municipal road does not have: an owner whose customers judge the property on how it looks.

The pedestrian crossings at Toronto Premium Outlets have been in service for **fourteen years**.

## What is actually there

TrafficPatternsXD: aggregate-reinforced preformed thermoplastic, 150 mil, heat-fused into the asphalt.

The pattern reads as laid masonry — the look of pavers, stones and cobbles — which is doing real work in a retail setting. It marks the pedestrian route with something a driver registers as *different ground*, not just a painted line, and it does it in a material that survives the traffic a parking lot actually generates.

The aggregate is rated 8–9 on the Mohs scale and runs through the full cross-section, so the surface has not gone slick where the wheel paths cross it. Skid resistance is specified at 60 BPN under ASTM E303.

## The number that matters to an owner

Fourteen years of constant use, and the crossings have provided a safe and durable path for pedestrians the whole time.

Set that against the alternative most commercial properties default to. Painted crosswalk markings in a busy retail lot are typically a repaint cycle measured in a season or two — which means recurring cost, recurring disruption, and a property that spends part of every year looking neglected.

Fourteen years is not a warranty and it is not a lab figure. It is one installation, still in service, that anyone can go and stand on.

## Where this applies

The same argument holds anywhere pedestrians and vehicles share a commercial surface:

- Retail centre crossings and pedestrian channelization
- Hotel and mixed-use forecourts
- Hospital and campus drop-offs
- Loading aprons and thresholds where wear concentrates

Whether it is worth specifying comes down to how much a repaint cycle costs you — in money, in closures, and in how the property reads to the people who use it.
